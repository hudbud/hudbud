Case study 1 — 2025

Fifteen oranges to one

LightSource had a logo that lived as a PNG people emailed each other, a brand color called "orange" with no hex code, and a website built from templates in purple. Before I could build a design system, the company needed an identity to build it from. So the rebrand came first.

Role
Led the rebrand end to end: identity, palette, type, voice, brand book, website design, sales collateral. Brand book co-authored with Gilbert Cordial.
Before
A Fiverr logo styled like an app icon, 15+ orange hex codes across materials, no typeface, no voice, no guidelines anyone could find.
After
Reconstructed logo, one orange (#FF4B00), Wasa and Inter, a 27-page brand book published to Notion, new website, new decks and one-pagers.
Brief
Confident, bold, cool. The CEO wanted a hint of Westworld and Star Wars: retro-futurism, taken in stride rather than literally.
What I found

A name with a logo, not a brand

The old brand was a logo. It had been designed by someone on Fiverr, stylized to look like an App Store icon with a little border around it, and it existed as a PNG that people passed around and asked each other for whenever they needed it. The brand color was "orange," in quotes. Nobody had defined a hex. When I audited the materials, I found more than fifteen different orange hex codes in use across the company.

The marketing website was devoid of brand: random colors, Tailwind templates, purple. There was no voice to speak of. So the job was bigger than a refresh. We needed to reconstruct the logo so it could scale and point in a direction. We needed a strong, reusable palette and an actual typeface. And, more importantly, we needed the company's first brand positioning, messaging, tone and language: how LightSource shows up in the world.

What we wanted the new brand to say was that we're confident, bold and cool. There was a lot of inspiration behind it. The CEO asked for something inspired by Westworld and Star Wars, a kind of retro-futurism, and we took that in stride without trying to implement it literally; we didn't have the resources to build a world. What we could do was move confidently: a bright, blazing orange against high-contrast black and white, to stand for emergence from consistency.

Color

The most orange a screen can be

We wanted an orange that was boldly orange, at the highest saturation a computer display can produce. We landed on #FF4B00. Technically it's a reddish orange, but it reads as orange, and more importantly it pops on a display. We're a software company; a screen is how we show up. Along the way we rejected the purples and periwinkles the old site had drifted toward, and a lot of clementine and sherbet oranges that felt soft.

The black took its own study. True black on true white is too much contrast, and we already had a true white. The orange sat better on a near-black, and we tested warmer and cooler versions before settling on #262626 and keeping it as neutral as possible. The orange is so fiery that the black's job is to stay out of its way.

Type

Wasa for display, Inter for everything else

The old type was Poppins, a geometric sans, and we didn't want to diverge too far from where the company had been. We explored a lot: serifs, other geometric sans-serifs, a wide field of rejects. We stayed sans.

Wasa got us close to Inter, which is essentially a Helvetica clone, while still being pairable with it; it wasn't so close that the two collapsed into each other. Wasa has futuristic glyphs and a modern tone, but classic weight and balance, and Wasa Bold felt full when it was set in the brand orange. Inter carries the body. Together they let the display type do the personality and the body type get out of the way.

Getting a company to adopt it

Work like an agency, lead with the work

A three-year-old company with no marketing team doesn't adopt a brand because a deck told it to. So we approached it the way an agency would: led with the work, took feedback in stride, and brought every asset the company could possibly need, so there was no excuse not to use branded material.

It started with the company's first brand book, 27 pages of guidelines that Gilbert Cordial and I built internally and then published as a Notion document so anyone could find it. From the book we rebuilt the core sales deck, several times over, which is where the brand voice really got defined, and turned it into templates. We designed new one-pagers for every salesperson, so the reusable assets existed before anyone had to ask. The website was the biggest sweep: a full redesign in Figma, then implemented in Framer.

Booths were in flux while the brand was still finding its confidence; the fall event used a Soviet-inspired design while we were still working the details out. By the time the brand book was done, everything the company put in front of a customer came from the same system.

We also spot-checked the brand against what the new product UI could look like, but the brand was the priority at that stage. The product interpretation came next, and it's the second case study.

Inspiration

High design, rusty western, Westworld, renegade

Those four phrases were the keywords for the brand. They live on a Cosmos board that collected the references: industrial materials, western landscapes, hard light, and the kind of confident typography that doesn't ask permission. The photo board at the bottom of the Figma canvas is the same idea applied to the brand film, which is still in progress.
