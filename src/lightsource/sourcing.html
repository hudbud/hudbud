Case study 3 — late 2025 to 2026

Rethinking the workflow, not just the screens

A sourcing project is the whole ecosystem for sending and receiving quotes, and it's where LightSource buyers spend most of their day. In classic, all of it lived on one Overview page. We rebuilt it as a workspace: Overview as mission control, items and suppliers promoted to first-class citizens, an analysis toolkit, and nothing that spits you out of the project.

The workflow

Customer interview

This is the workflow as a buyer lives it. Almost everything in this case study is about steps three through eight, and about the fact that the old UI treated the two most important nouns in it, items and suppliers, as second-class citizens.

  1. Bring items inFrom the ERP, another system, or just Excel, onto LightSource.
  2. Organize into BOMsAssemble items into bills of materials as needed with BOM management.
  3. Create a sourcing projectName it, set delivery date and location, and add items: from a BOM, or from scratch, as many as you want.
  4. Add suppliersA few or dozens. Then build the custom quote format they'll fill in.
  5. Send the RFQThe request for quote goes out to every supplier on the project.
  6. Quotes come backInto a quote management view organized by supplier.
  7. Analyze and optimizeBid comparison, bid ranking, and award optimization across suppliers on any criteria, with AI assistance.
  8. Submit the awardAward to one supplier or several.
  9. Reopen or resourceKeep sourcing the project, or duplicate it and run it again.
Information architecture

Overview as mission control

We broke the Overview into tabs because every one of these features deserved its own full-screen experience. That decision came out of a massive design exercise rethinking the sourcing workflow completely: how do you move through this meaningfully and comfortably? What we wanted was to surface the important parts of the workflow instead of piling them up.

Overview still has everything, but as a high-level mission control. Six tiles put items, suppliers, response rate, baseline, best quote and savings in one row; supplier status and a compact milestone strip sit beneath; recent activity replaces the long timeline. Going into any tab gives you granular control: Items for what's being sourced, Suppliers for who's quoting and what they've sent, Quote format for how they'll answer, Award for the decision.

The biggest shift is what moved from buried to front and center. Items and suppliers had been treated as secondary; they're the two key elements of the entire process. The items are what's getting sourced. The suppliers are the people you're doing it with. Each now has a page that treats it that way.

Suppliers and supplier detail

/quote-package/… → /suppliers/review/…
New tabSide panel

Classic had an RFQ timeline on the Overview and opened each supplier's quote package as a full page. Nova has a Suppliers tab with participation, response rate and quote status up top, and opens the supplier in a panel over the list. We had too much, and I wanted to keep the user in the project. Too many places in the old UI spat you out into a random part of the app; the rolodex is for managing suppliers globally, not in the context of this project. The panel previews everything without leaving the page.

Analysis

/bid-comparison/details → /suppliers/analyze/…
Toolkit, not a toolBeta

Classic bid comparison was sloppy UI in a standalone full-screen tool. Bringing it up to the new design language and making it scannable was table stakes. The bigger change is what it's becoming: an analysis toolkit rather than a fixed report. Bid comparison, bid ranking, flattened view and data explorer are the named views today, but the point is that you can generate any slice of the data your company needs, by asking the AI or by building it manually. It's under Suppliers while we beta test it; it's getting its own tab.

Process

Months on the whiteboard, with engineers

The photos are dated from their metadata, and the dates tell the story: this ran from October 2025 into spring 2026, in sessions with the engineers who would build it. Approval flows in October. An engineer sketching the quote-format builder in November. Messaging and the package view in January. Award paths in early March. The full end-to-end map in late March. Layout passes in April.

Customer sessions ran alongside, with buyers at aerospace, medical device and automotive companies. They taught us what people actually do in the software: they want the tool to work for them and be fast. They also taught us how wide the spectrum of sourcing practice is. Every company runs projects differently, so the interface has to be robust and agnostic to any one company's essential workflow.

Where it landed

Opt-in, on purpose

We shipped it as an opt-in toggle. We have too many users to flip things over instantly, and I didn't want to disturb anyone's flow. Opting in also let us run it as a beta: collect early feedback, see what's working and what isn't, and make sure we're building something that actually accelerates the workflow rather than just redesigning it without using what users tell us.

What still bugs me. I'd love this to be a step-by-step wizard, and it can't be, for exactly the reason above: there is no one way companies run a sourcing project. The tool is semi-nondeterministic, because people will use it however they want. So the best version of it brings every tool to the front without overwhelming anyone, makes each one easy, and lets the buyer work the way that fits their company. That's a harder design problem than a wizard, and it's the one I'm proudest of taking on.
