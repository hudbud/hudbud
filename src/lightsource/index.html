Redesigning LightSource, from the brand down

I joined a three-year-old procurement platform as its first designer: nineteen engineers, four salespeople, no marketing, and a product where every feature had been built in a vacuum. Over eighteen months I gave the company a brand, gave the product a design system, and rebuilt its core workflow. This is the umbrella for three case studies.

The starting point

A product that kept surprising you

When I joined, the interface was all over the place. There were no established patterns. Buttons, backgrounds and type used a seemingly random set of colors, and the primary call to action landed somewhere different on every page. Some flows opened modals, some opened slide-overs, some took over the full page. There were full-page experiences that should have been modals, modals that deserved a full page, and small sections that deserved a full page of their own.

Most importantly, the design language was so inconsistent that there was no learning curve. The platform surprised you with every feature in the workflow, so nothing you learned on one screen helped you on the next.

It got that way honestly. LightSource had been engineering-only for three years. Every feature had been built from real customer feedback, and the platform had grown organically because of it. The cost was that each feature was built on its own, by engineers focused on making the thing work, without anyone looking at patterns across the app. There was a design system on paper, but it had drifted into a free-for-all rather than a limited set of tokens and components.

I worked inside that paradigm for a few months while I did the brand redesign, and then built the design system from the brand. The order mattered, and the next section is about why.

Who it's for

Two kinds of buyer, one tool

LightSource replaces the Excel workflow for direct materials sourcing: quoting, supplier collaboration and analysis in one place instead of spreadsheets and email. A buyer's day, ideally, happens almost entirely inside it, mostly in sourcing projects, with BOM cost management as the other heavy path.

The people using it split two ways. Legacy buyers at established manufacturers run high volumes or strategic sourcing, are not especially comfortable with high-tech applications, and want something that just gets the job done. NPI buyers at new-age hardware companies are bringing products to market startup-style; they're young, comfortable with software, and want a tool that gives them superpowers.

Designing for both at once is the constraint that runs through all three case studies. The answer was never to pick one. It was to make every capability available without making any of it loud.

Why this order

Measure twice, cut once

The first thing the company asked me for was a new design system. I asked to see the brand guidelines. Someone pointed me at a Notion page with little to nothing on it; they weren't sure it existed. So I proposed doing the brand first.

A design system is only as good as the decisions underneath it, and I didn't want to encode a palette and a typeface into hundreds of components and then change them. The rebrand took a few months. Once it was done, the system fell into place far more easily: an airy, white product with a limited, deliberate use of a very bright orange. Brand should be reinforced at every moment a user interacts with the company. The more we do that, the more confidence and trust we build. So the brand constrained the design system, and that was by design.

The sourcing project redesign came last, because it needed both: a stable visual language to build in, and the freedom to rethink the workflow itself rather than restyle it.

How I design

Limit the toolkit, then take the time

I start from a core set of fundamentals and deliberately limit the creative toolkit, so that the time goes into making highly complex interactions feel straightforward. That's been the biggest exercise of this work: taking features that bring a lot of technicality to the user, pulling that technicality away, removing the noise, and making the workflow simpler while keeping every bit of the technical capability.

What makes this hard is that LightSource is an open-ended tool. There is no single workflow; every company sources differently, and people will use it however they want. The best you can do is bring every tool to the front without overwhelming anyone, make each one easy, and let the user work the way that fits their company.

Along the way I coordinated myself, two other designers and a team of about twenty engineers into this way of thinking. Feedback got more meaningful once more thought went into designs before they shipped. I'm strict about staying inside a small set of patterns, and I think that keeps paying off: it's a foundation to build features on, and it equips designers and engineers to think about UI with more empathy for the person on the other side.
